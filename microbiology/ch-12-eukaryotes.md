## Summary

This lecture is the eukaryotic microbes unit. Slide 3 reprints the prokaryote vs eukaryote comparison and says you are **responsible for this information**: one circular chromosome vs paired membrane-bounded chromosomes; no histones vs histones; no membrane organelles vs membrane organelles; peptidoglycan (bacteria) vs polysaccharide walls; binary fission vs mitotic spindle.

**Eukaryotic “microbes”** named: fungi, algae, protozoa, and helminths. Helminths are multicellular animals (worms). They are “not really considered microbes,” but they are studied here compared with animal and plant cells.

### Fungi

Two forms: unicellular **yeasts** and multicellular **molds**. The slide asks for examples and does not fill them in on that slide. Most are saprophytic. Benefits named: decompose dead plants, mycorrhizae, food (mushrooms), medicines (antibiotics).

Terms to know:

- Vegetative structures: **thallus** (body), **hyphae** (filaments)
- **Septate hyphae** vs **coenocytic hyphae**
- **Mycelium** = mass of filaments
- **Pseudohyphae** = incomplete budding, no cytoplasmic connection
- Life cycle: asexual or sexual reproduction; involves **spores** (for reproduction). Contrast with bacterial **endospores** (for survival)
- Three medically important phyla: Zygomycota, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota

**Yeasts** are unicellular fungi. Budding yeasts divide asymmetrically. **Dimorphic fungi** exist in two forms: yeast-like at 37°C, mold-like at 25°C. Example: *Candida albicans* — candidiasis. Superficial: oral thrush and vaginitis. Systemic: candidemia, life-threatening in immunocompromised persons.

**Problems from pathogenic fungi**

| Category | Slide wording / examples |
| --- | --- |
| Mycoses | Infection of body tissues. Aspergillosis, tinea (ringworm), *C. albicans* (mucous membranes, e.g. vaginitis), *Cryptococcus neoformans* (meningitis) |
| Mycotoxicoses | Fungal secondary metabolites toxic at low doses (aflatoxins, fumonisins) |
| Hypersensitive allergic reactions | Farmer’s lung (*Aspergillus*) |

Fungi produce **keratinase** (degrades keratin in skin, nails, and hair).

**Types of mycoses**

- **Cutaneous (dermatophytes):** tinea infections of skin, hair, nails (ringworm). *Trichophyton mentagrophyte* (skin, nails, hair); *Microsporum canis* (skin, nails, hair; major cause of ringworm; tinea capitis and tinea corporis); *Epidermophyton floccosum* (skin and nails). Tinea pedis = athlete’s foot. Tinea cruris = upper thigh in males.
- **Subcutaneous:** infections below the skin
- **Systemic:** spread throughout the body

The MT2 review lists dermatophytes under Chapter 7 by mistake. On the slides they belong here. Athlete’s foot on these slides is a dermatophyte (tinea pedis), not *Candida*.

### Protozoa

About 65,000 species. Many have complex life cycles and may need several hosts. **Definitive host:** host in which the parasite matures or reaches the reproductive stage. **Intermediate host:** harbors a developmental stage (e.g. larval). Forms: **trophozoite** or **cyst**.

Asexual reproduction: fission, budding, or **schizogony** (multiple fission; nucleus divides many times before the cell divides).

| Organism | Slide points |
| --- | --- |
| *Giardia lamblia* | Can form a cyst. Infects intestines — giardiasis (“Beaver fever”) |
| *Trichomonas vaginalis* | Trichomoniasis (STI). Anaerobic flagellated protozoan. No cyst form; skin-to-skin transmission. Most common pathogenic protozoan in industrialized countries. Women usually symptomatic; men usually asymptomatic. Treatment: metronidazole |
| *Toxoplasma gondii* | Felids (cats) = definitive hosts. Warm-blooded animals = intermediate hosts. Oocyst (developmental, with zygote); cyst (survival). Oocysts release sporozoites → tachyzoites (invade tissue). Bradyzoites = slow division. Can invade brain, eyes, fetus (cleaning cat litter) |
| *Plasmodium* (malaria) | Affects 300 million people/year; kills more people than HIV in Sub-Saharan Africa. Definitive host: *Anopheles* mosquito. Intermediate host: humans |

### Helminths

Characterized by reduced or lacking digestive system, reduced nervous system, reduced locomotion, and complex reproduction. **Dioecious** (separate male and female) or **monoecious** (both sexes; hermaphrodites). Egg → larva(e) → adult. Flatworms: tapeworms and flukes. Nematodes: roundworms, pinworm.

**Tapeworms:** adult in human intestine (oral–fecal route). Eggs in feces → eaten by pigs or cattle → larvae lodge in muscle → undercooked meat → adult worms attach with hooks and shed proglottids. Human pathogens (definitive hosts):

| Species | Slide wording |
| --- | --- |
| *Taenia saginata* | Beef tapeworm; can grow up to 6 m |
| *Taenia solium* | Pork tapeworm; consumed eggs hatch; larvae lodge in tissues (brain, muscle, or eyes) |
| *Diphyllobothrium latum* | Fish tapeworm |

**Roundworms:** trichinosis / trichinellosis from raw or undercooked pork or wild game with *Trichinella* larvae (*Trichinella spiralis*). Pinworms are named with “In-the-clinic” and are not described further on these slides.

The review asks for vaginosis vs vaginitis and Gram stains for bacterial vs yeast vaginal infection. **Bacterial vaginosis** (*Gardnerella*) is on the bacteria lecture. **Yeast vaginitis** (*Candida*) is here. These Chapter 12 slides do not write the Gram-stain difference sentence.
