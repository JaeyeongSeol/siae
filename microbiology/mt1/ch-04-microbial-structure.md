## Summary

The three-domain system is Carl Woese, 1978, again. This lecture bases the groups on ribosomal RNA genes. Bacteria are pathogenic and non-pathogenic prokaryotes. Archaea are prokaryotes that live in extreme environments and carry out unusual metabolism, for example extreme halophiles and hyperthermophiles. Eukarya includes animals, plants, and fungi.

Prokaryotes have one circular chromosome that is not membrane bounded, no histones, no membrane-bounded organelles, and peptidoglycan cell walls in bacteria. They divide by binary fission. Eukaryotes have paired chromosomes inside a membrane, histones, membrane-bounded organelles, and polysaccharide cell walls. They divide using a mitotic spindle.

Table 4.2 adds size, flagella, ribosomes, and division. Prokaryotes are typically 0.2–2.0 μm in diameter. The shape slide says average bacterial size is 0.2–1.0 μm × 2–8 μm. Eukaryotes are typically 10–100 μm in diameter. Prokaryotic flagella consist of two protein building blocks. Eukaryotic flagella are multiple microtubules. Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S. Eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S, with 70S in organelles. Prokaryotic sexual recombination on the table is “None; transfer of DNA only.” Eukaryotes use meiosis.

Basic shapes: coccus, bacillus, spirillum, spirochetes. Cocci divide in one or more planes: pairs (diplococci), chains (streptococci), clusters (staphylococci). Bacilli divide in one plane: diplobacilli and streptobacilli. Bacillus is both a genus name and a shape word. Vibrio is curved (*Vibrio cholerae*). Spirillum is a rigid corkscrew moved by external flagella. A spirochete is a flexible helix moved by an internal axial filament. Most bacteria are monomorphic (one shape). A few are pleomorphic (many shapes), for example *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*.

The glycocalyx is external to the cell wall, usually sticky, and is an extracellular polysaccharide for attachment. A capsule is neatly organized and tightly packed and prevents phagocytosis. A slime layer is unorganized and loose. *Streptococcus pneumoniae* is the capsule example.

Flagella are outside the cell wall, made of chains of flagellin, attached to a protein hook, and anchored by the basal body. Rotation can be clockwise or counterclockwise. Figure 4.7 names peritrichous, monotrichous and polar, lophotrichous and polar, and amphitrichous and polar. The slide does not write a sentence definition of each name. It also names runs and tumbles, chemotaxis, and random walk. The quiz key for “run” and “tumble” is cell mobility in response to attractants or repellents. A tuft at one end was scored as lophotrichous.

Fimbriae allow attachment. Pili are used to transfer DNA from one cell to another. The quiz also says pili are for DNA transfer and for cell motility. Motility is not in the slide sentence.

The cell wall gives shape and prevents osmotic lysis. Gram-positive walls are peptidoglycan: repeating NAG-NAM units cross-linked by polypeptides, plus teichoic acid and lipoteichoic acid. Penicillin blocks peptidoglycan cross-linking. Lysozyme breaks peptidoglycan. Gram-negative walls are an outer membrane plus a peptidoglycan layer. They protect against phagocytes, complement, and antibiotics. LPS includes Lipid A, which is an endotoxin, and an O-polysaccharide antigen such as *E. coli* O157:H7. Porins are proteins that form channels.

Mycoplasmas lack cell walls, are pleomorphic, and have sterols in the plasma membrane. Acid-fast walls are like Gram-positive walls plus a waxy lipid, mycolic acid (*Mycobacterium*, *Nocardia*).

The cytoplasm is the site of the cell’s biochemical activities and is 70–80% water. It contains the bacterial chromosome, ribosomes for protein synthesis, enzymes, and mRNA. Inclusions named on the slide include metachromatic granules (phosphate), polysaccharide granules, lipid inclusions, sulfur granules, carboxysomes, gas vacuoles, and magnetosomes (iron oxide, destroys H2O2).

Endospores are highly durable dehydrated dormant cells. They withstand boiling for about 20 hours. Sporulation is spore formation. Germination is the return to a vegetative cell. One spore gives rise to one bacterium. Species: *Bacillus anthracis*, *Clostridium tetani*, *C. botulinum*, *C. perfringens*, *C. difficile*.

Eukaryotic flagella and cilia are microtubules of tubulin in a 9 pairs + 2 array. The eukaryotic plasma membrane is a lipid bilayer with selective permeability. Internal structures named, and not given functions: endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, lysosomes, mitochondria, chloroplasts. Eukaryotes usually divide by mitosis.

The quiz sort: plasma membrane and ribosomes are both. Binary fission, flagellin proteins, and circular chromosomes are prokaryotes only. Lysosomes are eukaryotes only.

## 2. High-priority points

Likely exam content. Several of these also appear in Chapters 1, 3, and 7.

- Prokaryote versus eukaryote is the first objective and a quiz sorting item.
- The wall with teichoic acid is Gram-positive and is the wall sensitive to penicillin. The wall with Lipid A is Gram-negative. Both walls protect against osmotic lysis.
- Fimbriae attach. The slide sentence for pili is DNA transfer. The quiz phrase is “transfer DNA and for cell motility.”
- A tuft of flagella at one end was scored as lophotrichous. “Run” and “tumble” were scored as cell mobility toward or away from a chemical cue.
- Endospores return in Chapter 7 as the most resistant bacterial form. Boiling does not kill them. One spore becomes one cell.
- Acid-fast walls and mycolic acid connect this chapter to the acid-fast stain and to chemical resistance.
- Functions of the ER, Golgi, lysosomes, mitochondria, and chloroplasts are asked and not answered. Do not fill them in from outside the slides.
