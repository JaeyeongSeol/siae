## Summary

**Chemotherapy** is the use of drugs to treat a disease. **Antimicrobial drugs** interfere with growth of microbes within a host. An **antibiotic**, on the slide’s definition, is a substance produced by a microbe that, in small amounts, kills or inhibits another microbe. Fleming named antibiosis and later antibiotic after *Penicillium* inhibited *Staphylococcus aureus*. Florey and Chain ran the first clinical trials of penicillin.

**Paul Ehrlich** developed the concept of **selective toxicity** and discovered Agent 606, Salvarsan, for syphilis.

Sources: naturally occurring (Gram-positive rods / endospore formers; actinomycetes—*Streptomyces*; fungi—*Penicillium*), semisynthetic (ampicillin, amoxicillin), synthetic (trimethoprim). The review asks which group produces most antibiotics in use. The slides name *Streptomyces* (actinomycetes) and *Penicillium* as natural sources. They do **not** print a sentence that says which group produces “most.”

**Spectrum of activity** is the range of microbes a drug is effective against: narrow, broad, or extended. The slide asks advantages and disadvantages and does **not** answer.

**Therapeutic index (TI)** measures drug safety. A safe drug has a very high TI (lethal dose > effective dose). TI = TD50 / ED50. TD50 = dose that causes a toxic response in 50% of the population. ED50 = dose that is therapeutically effective in 50% of the population. LD50 and ID50 are named and not defined further.

**Five mechanisms of action** (Figure on slides 9 and 17):

1. Inhibition of cell wall synthesis — penicillins, cephalosporins, bacitracin, vancomycin  
2. Inhibition of protein synthesis — chloramphenicol, erythromycin, tetracyclines, streptomycin  
3. Inhibition of nucleic acid replication and transcription — quinolones, rifampin  
4. Injury to plasma membrane — polymyxin B  
5. Inhibition of essential metabolite synthesis — sulfanilamide, trimethoprim  

The figure also says the drug must not interfere with essential functions of the host (**selective toxicity**).

### Cell-wall inhibitors

**β-lactams** prevent cross-linking of peptidoglycans. Natural penicillins: Penicillin G, Penicillin V. Semisynthetic extended-spectrum: amoxicillin, ampicillin, carbenicillin. Penicillinase-resistant: oxacillin, methicillin. β-lactamase inhibitors can be added.

Clinical uses printed on the slide:

| Type | Uses named on the slide |
| --- | --- |
| Natural | Aerobic Gram-positive bacilli and anaerobic bacteria; *C. tetani*, *N. gonorrhoeae*, *N. meningitidis*, *T. pallidum* |
| Extended-spectrum semisynthetic | Non–β-lactamase producers; Gram-negative organisms and anaerobes; Gram-positive bacilli, *H. influenzae*, *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *V. cholerae*, *N. gonorrhoeae*, *N. meningitidis*, *Shigella* |
| Penicillinase-resistant | *S. aureus* and β-lactamase-producing organisms |

**Cephalosporins** are structurally similar to penicillin and are more widely used than other β-lactams. Grouped by generation (1st–4th). Bacteria have acquired β-lactamases that inactivate them.

**Polypeptide antibiotics:** Bacitracin inhibits linear peptidoglycan synthesis; topical; effective against Gram-positive. Colistin effective against Gram-negative, e.g. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. Vancomycin is a glycopeptide and an important “last line” against MRSA. Methicillin is no longer used or made, but MRSA still names penicillin-resistant *S. aureus*.

**Antimycobacterials:** Isoniazid (INH) inhibits mycolic acid synthesis. Ethambutol inhibits incorporation of mycolic acid into the cell wall. Can be given with isoniazid and rifamycin.

### Other modes

**Protein synthesis inhibitors** bind the small or large ribosomal subunit. Most are broad spectrum (the slide asks why and does not answer). Tetracyclines, macrolides (e.g. erythromycin), chloramphenicol, aminoglycosides (streptomycin, neomycin, kanamycin, gentamicin, tobramycin).

**DNA/RNA:** Rifamycin (rifampin) inhibits RNA synthesis; antimycobacterial (TB). Quinolones / fluoroquinolones: nalidixic acid was first (1962); ciprofloxacin (CIPRO) is broad-spectrum, synthetic, used for UTIs, publicized for anthrax.

**Plasma membrane:** Polymyxin B, topical, often combined with bacitracin and neomycin OTC; effective against Gram-negative such as *Pseudomonas*.

**Competitive enzyme inhibitors:** Sulfonamides (e.g. sulfamethoxazole) and trimethoprim inhibit folic acid synthesis. SXT = sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim (Bactrim, Septrin, Septra).

### Resistance

Mechanisms on the slide: prevent entrance of drug; pump out drug (efflux in *P. aeruginosa*); inactivation or degradation (β-lactamase / penicillinase); alteration of target (penicillin-binding proteins in MRSA); alternative pathways or more target metabolite (use of pre-formed folic acid).

Superbugs named: **MRSA** (some becoming vancomycin-resistant); **VRE** (vancomycin-resistant enterococci) — major nosocomial problem.

Factors: treating viral infections (common cold), expired antibiotics, antibiotics in animal feed, not completing prescription, leftover prescriptions, over-use, hospital-acquired resistance, agricultural practices, environmental contamination, globalization.

Prevention: use drugs only when necessary; high concentrations; two or more drugs at once; vaccines if available.

**Disk-diffusion (Kirby-Bauer):** measure zone of inhibition. **E-test:** minimum inhibitory concentration (**MIC**) = lowest antibiotic concentration preventing bacterial growth.

Antiviral drugs and “other antimicrobials” are labeled **FYI** on the slides.

## 4. Textbook notes (Tortora 14e)

No textbook PDF was available. These notes come from the textbook’s known content and were not checked against the 14e page text, so there are no page numbers.

For the exam, the slide wording comes first. Several MT2 review questions are answered in the book and not on the slides. Those answers are below.

### Review questions the book answers

1. **How the definition of antibiotic changed.** Originally, an antibiotic was a substance **produced by a microorganism** that, in small amounts, inhibits another microorganism. That is the slide’s definition. Today the word is used more loosely and also covers **semisynthetic and synthetic** drugs. The broad term is **antimicrobial drug**.
2. **Which group makes most antibiotics in use.** ***Streptomyces*** (actinomycetes). The book says that more than half of our antibiotics come from *Streptomyces* species. Other producers named in the book: *Bacillus*, and the fungi *Penicillium* and *Cephalosporium*.
3. **Narrow vs broad spectrum: advantages and disadvantages.**
   - Broad spectrum: useful when the cause is not yet known. Disadvantage: it also kills the **normal microbiota**, which can lead to a **superinfection**, an overgrowth of resistant organisms such as *Candida* or *C. difficile*.
   - Narrow spectrum: leaves more of the normal microbiota alone. Disadvantage: you need to know which organism you are treating.
4. **Why some drugs are bactericidal and others bacteriostatic.** Drugs that block **cell-wall synthesis** make the cell burst, so they kill (bactericidal). Most drugs that block **protein synthesis** only stop growth (bacteriostatic). If the drug is removed, the cells can grow again. **Aminoglycosides**, such as streptomycin, are an exception and are bactericidal.
5. **Drug choices for named organisms.** The review says to assume there is no resistance and to pick a cheap oral drug with few side effects. The table below is that reasoning, based on what the book says about each organism. Confirm the expected answers with your professor.

| Organism | Reasoning from the book |
| --- | --- |
| *Streptococcus pyogenes* | Penicillin. With no resistance, it is cheap and effective |
| *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | Penicillin, for the same reason |
| *Treponema pallidum* | Penicillin. The slide also lists *T. pallidum* under the natural penicillins |
| *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* | Penicillin if there is no resistance. The slide lists it under the penicillins. The book notes that resistance is widespread in practice, so cephalosporins are used |
| *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Isoniazid plus rifampin, with ethambutol. These are on the slide |
| *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* | **Not** a penicillin, because it has no cell wall. The book uses a macrolide (erythromycin or azithromycin) or tetracycline |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Penicillin if there is no resistance. With penicillinase, use a penicillinase-resistant penicillin. For MRSA, vancomycin |

### Where the book adds detail to the slide

- **Resistance genes:** the book explains that resistance genes are often carried on **plasmids (R factors)**. They spread between bacteria by conjugation, transduction, and transformation.
- **The five resistance mechanisms** in the book match the slide: destroying the drug with an enzyme, blocking entry, changing the target, pumping the drug out (efflux), and using a different metabolic pathway.
- **MBC (minimal bactericidal concentration):** the lowest concentration that **kills** the bacteria. MIC, on the slide, is the lowest concentration that **inhibits** growth. MBC is not on the slides.
- **Combination therapy:** the book describes **synergism**, where two drugs together work better than either alone, and **antagonism**, where one drug interferes with the other. The slide only says to use two or more drugs at once.

### Same in the slides and the book

Selective toxicity; spectrum terms; TI = toxic dose ÷ effective dose; the five modes of action; β-lactams block peptidoglycan cross-linking; vancomycin for MRSA; isoniazid and ethambutol act on mycolic acid; Kirby-Bauer zones; the MIC.
