Part 1 · Chapter 7 — Controlling Microbial Growth (L6)

The big idea We use heat, filters, cold, drying, radiation, and chemicals to kill microbes or stop them from growing. The words for each action (sterilize, disinfect, sanitize, antisepsis) depend on how many microbes are removed and where (object or living skin).

1. The key words — in plain English

TermWhat it means (slide wording)In plain EnglishSlide
Sterilization“Destruction/removal of all forms of microbial life from an object”Everything is dead or removed. Nothing alive is left.4
Disinfection“Use of chemicals (disinfectants) to kill or inhibit microorganisms that cause disease.” “Does not kill all microorganisms.” “Usually toxic and injurious to human tissues”Kills the disease-causing germs on objects, but not everything. Too harsh for skin.5
Sanitization“only reduces the microbial population to acceptable standards”Lowers the count to a safe level (for example, restaurant utensils).6
Antisepsis“Chemicals used to treat or prevent infection of living tissues (still toxic)”Chemicals on living skin or tissue.6
-cide“suffix indicating agent kills organisms”-cide = kills (biocide, germicide, fungicide, virocide).7
-static“agent that inhibit growth”-static = stops growth but does not kill (bacteriostatic).7
Sepsis“Bacterial contamination”Bacteria are present where they should not be.7
Asepsis“Absence of significant contamination”Clean, no important contamination. Aseptic technique keeps it that way.7
Thermal death point“Lowest temperature that kills all microbes in liquid culture in 10 minutes.”The temperature (time fixed at 10 min).10
Thermal death time“Length of time required to kill all bacteria in liquid culture at given temperature.”The time (temperature fixed).10
Decimal reduction time“Length of time taken to kill 90% of bacterial population at given temperature.”Time to kill 90% (one “decimal”).10
Easy way to remember Sterilize = all gone. Disinfect = objects, disease germs. Antisepsis = skin (living). Sanitize = safe level. -cide kills, -static stops.

2. What changes how fast microbes die

  • Number of microbes: more microbes take longer to kill.
  • Type of microbes: some are more sensitive than others.
  • Concentration / intensity: slide 8 says a higher concentration has a greater effect.
  • Time: longer exposure kills more.
  • Environment: heat increases killing. Organic matter (sewage, biofilms) decreases killing.
  • Microbe features: spores are more resistant than vegetative (active) cells.

3. Who is hardest to kill? (most → least resistant)

Prions → bacterial endospores → mycobacteria → protozoan cysts → vegetative protozoa → Gram-negative bacteria → fungi → naked viruses (no envelope) → Gram-positive bacteria → enveloped viruses

  • Gram-negative is harder to kill than Gram-positive. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most resistant Gram-negative.
  • Acid-fast (Mycobacterium) is harder to kill than non-acid-fast.
  • Naked viruses are harder to kill than enveloped viruses.
  • Cysts are harder to kill than trophozoites. Cryptosporidium cysts resist chlorine in water.

4. Physical methods

MethodHow it works / numbers to know
Moist heatKills by coagulating protein. More effective than dry heat. Steam beats boiling water. Endospores survive boiling.
AutoclaveSteam under pressure: 121°C, 15 psi, 15 minutes. Kills C. botulinum spores in canning.
Dry heatKills by oxidation. Needs higher heat and more time. Oven 170°C for 2 hours; flaming; incineration.
PasteurizationReduces pathogens and does not change taste. Milk target = Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
LTH 62.8°C for 30 min · HTST 72°C for 15 sec · UHT 74°C → 140°C → 74°C in 5 sec
FiltrationFor liquids that heat would damage (heat-labile). Depth and membrane filters.
Cold / drying / salt or sugarBacteriostatic (stops growth only). Listeria grows in the fridge. Molds and yeasts handle drying and salt best.
RadiationUV = non-ionizing, does not penetrate, damages DNA and eyes. Ionizing (gamma, X-ray, electron beams) causes mutations and death; sterilizes medical supplies.

5. Chemical methods

ChemicalKey facts from the slides
PhenolUsed by Joseph Lister. Lysol = mix of phenols. Triclosan banned in US soaps (2017).
ChlorhexidineSkin and mucous membranes; used by dentists; low toxicity.
IodineTincture (with alcohol) or iodophor (Betadine).
ChlorineWater disinfection. Active form = hypochlorous acid. Weakened by organic matter.
AlcoholsDenature proteins and damage membranes. 70% works better than 100%.

Disc method for disinfectants: spread bacteria on a plate → add discs soaked in disinfectant → incubate → measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition). Bigger zone = stronger effect.

Slides vs textbook — Chapter 7
  • Sterilization: the book adds that it includes endospores. Prions may survive normal sterilization. The book also has commercial sterilization for canned food (enough heat to kill C. botulinum spores).
  • Disinfection: the book says it kills vegetative pathogens on nonliving objects.
  • Antisepsis: the book says it kills vegetative pathogens on living tissue.
  • Degerming is only in the book: removing microbes from a small area (alcohol swab before an injection, handwashing).
  • Disc test: the slide calls it the “disc dilution method.” The book calls it the disk-diffusion method and separately describes a use-dilution test.
  • Answers the book gives: 70% alcohol works better because water is needed to denature proteins. Mycobacteria resist because of their waxy mycolic acid wall. Naked viruses resist because many chemicals attack the lipid envelope, which they lack. Liquids in an autoclave boil over if the pressure drops quickly.
  • Same in both: moist heat coagulates protein, dry heat oxidizes; autoclave 121°C / 15 psi / 15 min; HTST 72°C / 15 sec; the resistance order.

Part 2 · Chapter 20 — Antibiotics (L7)

The big idea Antibiotics are drugs that kill or stop microbes inside the body without hurting the patient (selective toxicity). Each drug attacks one of five targets in the bacterium. Bacteria fight back with resistance.

1. The key words

TermSlide wordingIn plain EnglishSlide
Chemotherapy“Use of drugs to treat a disease”Treating disease with drugs.4
Antimicrobial drugs“Interfere with growth of microbes within a host”Drugs that work against microbes inside the body.4
Antibiotic“Substance produced by a microbe that, in small amounts, kills/inhibits another microbe”A microbe makes it, and it hurts another microbe.4
Selective toxicityEhrlich’s concept. The drug must not interfere with essential functions of the hostHarm the germ, not the patient.3, 9
Spectrum of activity“Range of microbes a drug is effective against”Narrow = few types. Broad = many types. Extended is also named.7
Therapeutic index (TI)TI = TD50 / ED50. “A safe drugs has a very high therapeutic index”Toxic dose ÷ effective dose. Bigger TI = safer.8
MIC“Lowest antibiotic concentration preventing bacterial growth”Smallest amount that stops growth (E-test).27
Kirby-Bauer“Measure zone of inhibition to determine the sensitivity of the organism to the antibiotic.”Disk test: bigger clear zone = bacteria more sensitive.26

2. History (who did what)

  • Fleming: Penicillium mold stopped S. aureus. He named antibiosis and later antibiotic.
  • Florey & Chain: first clinical trials of penicillin.
  • Paul Ehrlich: idea of selective toxicity; Salvarsan (“Agent 606”) for syphilis.

3. Where antibiotics come from

NaturalSemisyntheticSynthetic
Made by microbes: Streptomyces (actinomycetes), Penicillium (fungus), endospore-forming Gram-positive rodsNatural drug changed in a lab: ampicillin, amoxicillinMade completely in a lab: trimethoprim

4. The five ways antibiotics work

#TargetDrugs on the slides
1Cell wall synthesisPenicillins, cephalosporins, bacitracin, vancomycin
2Protein synthesis (ribosome)Chloramphenicol, erythromycin, tetracyclines, streptomycin (aminoglycosides)
3DNA / RNA synthesisQuinolones (ciprofloxacin), rifampin
4Plasma membrane injuryPolymyxin B
5Metabolite (folic acid) synthesisSulfanilamide / sulfonamides, trimethoprim (SXT = both together, Bactrim)
Memorize first
  • β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) block peptidoglycan cross-linking in the cell wall. β-lactamase (penicillinase) breaks them.
  • Vancomycin = “last line” against MRSA.
  • TB drugs: isoniazid (INH) blocks mycolic acid synthesis; ethambutol blocks putting mycolic acid into the wall; rifampin blocks RNA synthesis.
  • Polymyxin B and colistin work on Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas. Bacitracin works on Gram-positive bacteria (topical).

5. Antibiotic resistance

How bacteria resist (5 ways):

  1. Block entry — the drug cannot get in.
  2. Pump it out — efflux pumps (P. aeruginosa).
  3. Destroy the drug — β-lactamase / penicillinase.
  4. Change the target — changed penicillin-binding proteins in MRSA.
  5. Use another pathway — for example, use ready-made folic acid.

Superbugs: MRSA (methicillin-resistant S. aureus) and VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococci), a big problem in hospitals.

Why resistance increases: antibiotics for viral infections (common cold), not finishing the prescription, leftover or expired pills, antibiotics in animal feed, overuse, hospitals, globalization.

How to prevent it: use antibiotics only when needed, use high concentrations, use two or more drugs together, and use vaccines when available.

Asked on the slides or review but not answered on L7 How the antibiotic definition has changed · which group makes most antibiotics · pros and cons of narrow vs broad spectrum · why some drugs are bactericidal and others bacteriostatic · a first-choice drug for S. pyogenes, S. pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae. The textbook answers are in the red box.
Slides vs textbook — Chapter 20
  • Antibiotic definition: the slide gives the original definition (made by a microbe). The book says that today the word also covers semisynthetic and synthetic drugs. The broad term is “antimicrobial drug.”
  • Who makes most antibiotics: the book says Streptomyces makes more than half.
  • Narrow vs broad: broad is useful when the cause is unknown, but it kills normal flora and can cause a superinfection (for example Candida or C. difficile). Narrow spares normal flora, but you must know the organism.
  • Bactericidal vs bacteriostatic: cell-wall drugs make the cell burst, so they kill. Most protein-synthesis drugs only stop growth. Exception: aminoglycosides (streptomycin) kill.
  • Extra terms in the book: R plasmids (R factors) carry resistance genes between bacteria. MBC = lowest concentration that kills (MIC only stops growth). Synergism = two drugs work better together. Antagonism = one drug blocks the other.

Drug choices (review question; assume no resistance, cheap, oral, few side effects): this is reasoning from the book. Confirm the expected answers with your professor.

Part 3 · Chapters 10 & 11 — Bacteria (L8)

The big idea Bacteria are prokaryotes (domain Bacteria). We identify them with tests (shape, Gram stain, biochemistry, DNA) and classify them into groups. You need to know the main pathogens in each group.

1. How do we identify bacteria?

  • Shape and cell wall (morphology)
  • Staining — Gram stain, capsule stain
  • Biochemical tests — which enzymes the bacterium has
  • Serology — antibodies (slide agglutination, ELISA)
  • DNA — % G+C, sequencing, rRNA. (G+C + A+T = 100%. Closely related organisms have similar G+C.)
ToolSlide wordingIn plain EnglishSlide
Dichotomous key“Identification keys based on successive questions.” “Each question has two possible answers”A yes/no flowchart to identify an unknown bacterium.7–8
Cladogram“Maps that show evolutionary relationships among organisms; based on rRNA/DNA sequences.”A family tree showing who is related to whom.7, 10
Bergey’s Manual“provides identification schemes for identifying members of bacteria and archaea Domains.”The reference book for identifying bacteria.4

2. Naming and ranking

Ranks (small → big): Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum → Kingdom → Domain

RankName ending (new rules)Example
Kingdom-atiPseudomonadati
Phylum-otaPseudomonadota
Class-iaGammaproteobacteria

Family-aceaeEnterobacteriaceae
Genus + species—Escherichia coli
  • Binomial name (Linnaeus): Genus species. Genus starts with a capital letter, species is lowercase, and both are italic (or underlined).
  • Write the genus in full the first time, then abbreviate (E. coli). Reason: Escherichia coli and Entamoeba coli are different organisms.
  • Prokaryotic species = population of cells with similar characteristics. Eukaryotic species = organisms that interbreed. Clone = cells from one single cell. Strain = genetically different cells within a species.

Part 4 · Chapter 12 — Eukaryotic Microbes (L9)

The big idea Four kinds of eukaryotic “microbes” cause disease: fungi, algae, protozoa, and helminths (worms — not really microbes, but included). Know the fungal terms, the types of fungal infection, and which host is definitive vs intermediate.

1. Prokaryote vs eukaryote (slide says you are responsible)

HistonesNoYes

DivisionBinary fissionMitotic spindle

2. Fungi — the words

Hyphae“filaments”6
Mycelium“mass of filaments”6

Easy way to remember Dimorphic: 37°C = body = yeast. 25°C = room = mold. Fungal spores = make babies. Bacterial endospores = survive bad times.

3. How fungi make us sick

ProblemMeaning and examples
Mycoses“Infection of body tissues.” Aspergillosis, tinea (ringworm), Candida, Cryptococcus (meningitis)
MycotoxicosesPoisoning by fungal toxins at low doses (aflatoxins)
HypersensitivityAllergy — farmer’s lung (Aspergillus)

Types of mycoses (slides list 3):

  • Cutaneous = skin, hair, nails. Caused by dermatophytes → ringworm (tinea): Trichophyton, Microsporum canis (major cause of ringworm), Epidermophyton. Tinea capitis (scalp), corporis (body), pedis (athlete’s foot), cruris (groin/thigh). Fungi use keratinase to break down keratin.
  • Subcutaneous = below the skin.
  • Systemic = spread throughout the body.

Candida albicans: dimorphic yeast. Superficial = oral thrush, vaginitis. Systemic = candidemia (dangerous in immunocompromised people). Athlete’s foot is tinea pedis (dermatophyte), not Candida.

4. Protozoa

  • Trophozoite = active form. Cyst = protective form.
  • Definitive host = where the parasite matures / reproduces. Intermediate host = holds a developmental (larval) stage.
  • Schizogony = multiple fission (the nucleus divides many times before the cell divides).
OrganismKey facts
Giardia lambliaForms cysts; intestinal infection (giardiasis, “beaver fever”)
Trichomonas vaginalisSTI; no cyst; women usually have symptoms, men usually not; treat with metronidazole
Toxoplasma gondiiCats = definitive host; warm-blooded animals = intermediate. Danger to fetus (cat litter in pregnancy), brain, eyes
Plasmodium (malaria)Anopheles mosquito = definitive host; humans = intermediate host

5. Helminths (worms)

  • Dioecious = separate male and female. Monoecious = both organs (hermaphrodite). Life cycle: egg → larva → adult.
  • Tapeworms (from undercooked meat): Taenia saginata = beef (up to 6 m); Taenia solium = pork (larvae can go to brain, muscle, eyes); Diphyllobothrium latum = fish.
  • Roundworms: Trichinella spiralis → trichinosis from raw pork or wild game. Pinworms are named only.
Slides vs textbook — Chapter 12
  • Types of mycoses: slides = 3 (cutaneous, subcutaneous, systemic). Book = 5, adding superficial (only the surface of hair and skin) and opportunistic (normally harmless fungi that cause disease in a weak host, e.g. Candida, Aspergillus).
  • Giardia name: slide = G. lamblia. Book = G. intestinalis (also called G. lamblia). Same organism.
  • Hosts: book says definitive host = where the parasite reaches sexual maturity; intermediate = larval or encysted stage. Same idea as the slide.
  • Book definitions of slide terms: septate hyphae have cross-walls (septa); coenocytic hyphae have no cross-walls (one long cell with many nuclei). Asexual spores come from one parent by mitosis; sexual spores come from two mating strains. Zygomycota → zygospores, Ascomycota → ascospores, Basidiomycota → basidiospores.
  • Candida: the book calls it an opportunistic yeast, often after antibiotics kill normal bacteria (superinfection, Ch 20). Like the slide, the book gives athlete’s foot to dermatophytes.
